How this calculator works

Pit storage is a cylinder volume converted to gallons; the cycle math is a fill-and-drain balance between pump and inflow:

gal per inch = π × (diameter ÷ 2)² × 1 ÷ 231 storage (gal) = gal per inch × float range (in) run time (min)= storage ÷ (pump GPM − inflow GPM) refill (min) = storage ÷ inflow GPM cycles/hour = 60 ÷ (run time + refill time)
liters per cm = π × (diameter ÷ 200)² × 0.01 × 1000 storage (L) = liters per cm × float range (cm) run time (min)= storage ÷ (pump L/min − inflow L/min) refill (min) = storage ÷ inflow L/min cycles/hour = 60 ÷ (run time + refill time)
⚠️ Estimates for planning only. Real pump output drops with vertical lift and pipe friction (a "40 GPM" pump may deliver 25 at your head). Size the pit for the worst storm of the year, and always run a check valve on the discharge line.

Pit design tips

  • Bigger pit, longer life. Doubling the float range doubles every cycle time — the cheapest way to spare your pump.
  • Set the float wide. Pump-off to pump-on of 8–12 inches is ideal; a 3-inch range means constant cycling.
  • Gravel bed under the pit and weep holes in the liner so ground water actually reaches the pump.
  • Two pumps in big rooms. If inflow exceeds ~30 GPM regularly, a second pump with a battery backup beats one giant pump.
  • Call before you dig. In the US dial 811 — free and required.

Frequently asked questions

What is the standard sump pit size?

Most residential sump pits are 18 to 24 inches in diameter and 24 to 36 inches deep. An 18-inch pit holds about 1.1 gallons per inch of depth and a 24-inch pit about 2 gallons per inch. Bigger is generally better: more storage means fewer pump starts and a longer pump life.

How many gallons does a sump pit hold?

Gallons equal π × (diameter in inches ÷ 2)² × water depth in inches ÷ 231. A typical 18-inch pit with 10 inches of water between pump-on and pump-off holds about 11 gallons; a 24-inch pit the same depth holds about 20 gallons.

How often should a sump pump cycle?

Keep it under 10 starts per hour, and ideally around 6. Each start draws a surge of current and wears the switch; a pit that cycles every couple of minutes needs either a bigger pit, a wider float gap, or a check that the inflow estimate is right.

What GPM sump pump do I need?

The pump must exceed your peak inflow with margin. A common 1/3 HP pump moves about 40 GPM at a 10-foot head; 1/2 HP moves about 60 GPM. Size for the worst storm, not the average day — and remember vertical lift and pipe friction cut the rated flow significantly.

Can a sump pit be too big?

Practically no. An oversized pit cycles less often, which extends pump and switch life. The only downsides are excavation cost and floor space. Too small is the real problem: rapid short cycling burns out pumps within a season or two.
