How this calculator works

This page solves the Manning equation for a circular pipe flowing full. You supply the diameter, the material (which sets the roughness coefficient n) and the slope; the calculator returns the capacity and the corresponding velocity:

US units: Q (cfs) = (1.486 / n) × A × R^(2/3) × S^(1/2) A = π × D² / 4 (D in ft) R = D / 4 (hydraulic radius, full pipe) S = slope as decimal (ft drop per ft run) gpm = Q × 448.8 V (ft/s) = Q / A
SI units: Q (m³/s) = (1 / n) × A × R^(2/3) × S^(1/2) A = π × D² / 4 (D in m) R = D / 4 (hydraulic radius, full pipe) S = slope as decimal (m drop per m run) L/s = Q × 1000 V (m/s) = Q / A

Velocity check (built into the results)

  • Below 2 ft/s (0.6 m/s): flow may be too slow to keep sediment moving — the pipe can silt up over time.
  • 2–10 ft/s (0.6–3 m/s): the healthy self-cleansing band most designs target.
  • Above 10 ft/s (3 m/s): erosion risk at bends and outlets; consider flattening the slope or stepping the diameter up.

Design tips

  • Doubling the diameter raises capacity about 5.7× (area scales with D², hydraulic radius with D) — upsizing is far more effective than steepening.
  • Slope matters less than you think: capacity scales with the square root of slope, so 4× steeper only doubles the flow.
  • Material choice is real capacity: at the same size and slope, smooth PVC (n = 0.009) carries ~60% more water than corrugated steel (n = 0.024).
  • Need the slope instead of the capacity? Use the pipe slope calculator. Sizing a basin inlet? See the catch basin calculator.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Manning equation?

The Manning equation estimates flow in open channels and partially or full pipes using gravity: Q = (1.486/n) × A × R^(2/3) × S^(1/2) in US units, where Q is flow, n is the roughness coefficient, A is cross-sectional area, R is hydraulic radius (D/4 for a full circular pipe) and S is slope. It is the standard method for sizing storm drains, culverts and sewer lines.

What Manning's n value should I use?

Typical values: PVC and HDPE with smooth interior 0.009–0.012, concrete pipe 0.013, vitrified clay 0.013, corrugated steel 0.024. Smoother pipe carries noticeably more water at the same slope — a PVC pipe at n = 0.009 flows about 40% more than corrugated steel at n = 0.024.

What velocity should a drainage pipe have?

Aim for at least 2 ft/s (0.6 m/s) so the flow keeps itself clean of sediment, and below about 10 ft/s (3 m/s) to avoid erosion and noise at outlets. Storm drains are usually designed for 3–6 ft/s.

How much water can a 4 inch pipe carry?

A 4 inch PVC pipe at 1% slope carries roughly 123 gpm at full flow with a velocity of about 3.2 ft/s. At 2% slope the same pipe carries about 174 gpm. Smoothness, slope and diameter all matter — use the calculator for your exact combination.

Is full-pipe capacity the real flow?

Gravity pipes rarely run full. Maximum discharge in a circular pipe actually occurs at about 94% of the diameter, giving roughly 8% more flow than the full-pipe figure. This calculator reports the conservative full-flow capacity, which is the standard design value.
