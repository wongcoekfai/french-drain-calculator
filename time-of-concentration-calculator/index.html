How this calculator works

The Kirpich equation (1940) estimates time of concentration from just two numbers — path length and slope:

US units: Tc (min) = 0.0078 × L^0.77 × S^-0.385 L = flow path length (ft) S = slope = elevation drop ÷ length (ft/ft) average velocity = L ÷ Tc
SI units: Tc (min) = 0.0195 × L^0.77 × S^-0.385 L = flow path length (m) S = slope = elevation drop ÷ length (m/m) average velocity = L ÷ Tc

Using Tc in the Rational Method

  1. Measure the longest (hydraulically most distant) flow path to your outlet — overland, down a swale, through a channel.
  2. Get Tc here.
  3. Read rainfall intensity i from your local IDF curve at duration = Tc.
  4. Compute peak flow Q = C × i × A — the catch basin calculator walks through that step.
⚠️ Kirpich assumes a single natural flow path and was calibrated on small rural watersheds. It can underestimate Tc on paved urban sites and overestimate it in very flat terrain — check which method your local stormwater manual prescribes.

Typical Tc ranges

  • Parking lot / roof catchment: 5–10 minutes — impervious surfaces shed water fast.
  • Residential yard or small swale: 10–20 minutes.
  • Multi-acre gentle site: 20–60 minutes.
  • Most jurisdictions set a minimum design Tc of 5 minutes — shorter results are clipped to 5.

Frequently asked questions

What is time of concentration?

Time of concentration (Tc) is the time it takes water to travel from the hydraulically most distant point of a catchment to the outlet. It is the key timing input for the Rational Method: rainfall intensity is read for a storm duration equal to Tc, because the whole catchment contributes flow at that moment.

What is the Kirpich equation?

Kirpich (1940) estimates Tc from channel length and slope: Tc = 0.0078 × L^0.77 × S^-0.385, with Tc in minutes, L in feet and S as slope in ft per ft. In SI units the coefficient is 0.0195 with L in meters. It was calibrated on small agricultural watersheds and works best for short overland and small-channel flow paths.

Why does a shorter Tc mean a bigger flood?

Rainfall intensity increases as storm duration decreases. A steep, compact catchment has a short Tc, so the design storm duration is short and the matching intensity is high — peak flow Q = C × i × A grows. Flatter, longer catchments have long Tc values matched to gentler, longer storms.

What is a typical time of concentration?

Small impervious areas concentrate fast: a parking lot or roof catchment often has Tc of 5–10 minutes, a residential yard 10–20 minutes, and a several-acre gently sloped site 20–60 minutes. Many jurisdictions set a minimum design Tc of 5 minutes.

When should I not use Kirpich?

Kirpich assumes a single natural flow path and tends to underestimate Tc in paved urban catchments and overestimate it in very flat terrain. For urban design many agencies prefer the FAA method or the SCS lag method, and segmented Tc (sheet + shallow concentrated + channel flow) for mixed paths.
